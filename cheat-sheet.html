1. Wage and purchasing reward

w = W/P

W = money wage/hour; P = goods price. w is pre-tax goods/hour.

R = [(1 − tn)/(1 + tc)] × w

R is what an extra work hour can buy after both taxes.

tn = labor tax; tc = consumption tax

Use decimal rates: 25% = 0.25. P excludes consumption tax.

10 × 0.75 = 7.5; 10 ÷ 1.25 = 8

Separate examples: 25% labor tax versus 25% consumption tax; w held at 10.

SRC-012 slide 26; SRC-083 pp. 2-4. Combined-tax expression: supplemental accounting.

2. Individual to aggregate hours

Ns(w) = Σi hi(w)

At one common wage, add each person's offered hours.

Ns = pop × h (equal hours only)

pop = working-age people; h = hours per person.

4 × 5 = 20 → 3 × 5 = 15

Fewer people; same hours per remaining person.

Reset: 4 × 5 = 20 → 4 × 4 = 16

Same people; fewer hours each as leisure becomes more attractive.

SRC-012 slides 21, 26, 35. Sum notation and small-group arithmetic: generated explanation.

3. Read the labor-market graph

Vertical: w = W/P; horizontal: N

N is total labor hours. Demand slopes down; course supply slopes up with lifetime resources fixed.

Nd(w*) = Ns(w*) = N*

The intersection gives the market wage w* and labor hours N*.

Same w + fewer supplied hours ⇒ supply LEFT

Wage change alone: move along supply. Supply shifters: lifetime resources (PVLR), taxes, population, value of leisure (VL).

A, K fixed ⇒ labor demand unchanged

A = productivity; K = total capital. Find the new intersection after identifying the supply shift.

SRC-012 slides 19-29; SRC-083 p. 2. Stars denote equilibrium.

4. Temporary and permanent taxes

Temporary tax ↑: Ns ←; N* ↓; w* ↑; R ↓

Ignore the lifetime-resource effect; hold A, K and other shifters fixed. No offsetting transfer is included.

Permanent tax ↑: SE ←; IE →

SE = lower reward makes leisure relatively cheaper. IE = fewer lifetime resources encourages more work, with normal leisure.

Final N*: lower, unchanged, or higher

The income effect pushes right RELATIVE TO substitution-only supply. Compare the strengths of both effects.

Exact offset: same N*, same w*; R lower

One possible case. The original intersection returns; the tax still applies.

SRC-012 slides 23, 25-27, 32-34; SRC-131 tax diagrams; SRC-083 pp. 2-6.

5. Population and leisure shifts

Working-age population ↓ ⇒ aggregate Ns ←

At the same wage, the remaining individual supply schedules can stay unchanged.

Value of leisure ↑ ⇒ individual hours ↓

If this change applies across households, aggregate supply shifts left too.

With demand fixed: N* ↓; w* ↑

Reset between scenarios. Hold taxes, lifetime resources and the other supply shifters fixed for each comparison.

SRC-012 slides 21, 26, 35; SRC-083 pp. 7-8, 11.

6. Output, potential output and LRAS

Y = A × K0.3 × N0.7

Y = real output; N = total labor hours.

Y* = A × K0.3 × (N*)0.7

N* = market-clearing hours. Potential is not a physical maximum.

Y/N = A × (K/N)0.3

Output per hour. Fixed A, K: N ↓ ⇒ Y ↓ but Y/N ↑.

LRAS: P vertical; Y horizontal; Y = Y*

P = price level. Flexible W: P ×2 and W ×2 leave W/P, N* and Y* fixed.

A, K or N* change ⇒ LRAS can shift

P alone does not shift LRAS; hold K fixed unless stated.

Sticky wage + hours demanded < hours supplied

Can put actual Y below current Y*. Lower clearing N* alone is not cyclical unemployment.

The supply curve separates the current substitution effect from lifetime resources. Temporary examples set the lifetime-resource effect aside.
