Model and units: All numerical wage examples have zero labor and consumption taxes. W is money pay per labor hour, P is a representative good’s price, and W/P is goods per labor hour. Lowercase n means one person’s desired work hours per day in this teaching illustration. Capital N adds people’s hours at the same wage. The lecture generally uses N; lowercase n is introduced only to make the distinction explicit. Labor supply is desired hours offered, not actual hours hired or LRAS output.

Controlled effects: One upward curve holds PVLR, taxes, population and leisure preferences fixed. Its slope isolates substitution. Higher PVLR at the same wage shifts supply left because leisure is normal in the course model. The temporary wage exercise treats PVLR changes as negligible. Permanent wage changes can also change PVLR, adding an opposing income effect; the net hours change needs a relative-strength assumption. A permanent productivity shock can make PVLR endogenous, which means determined within the larger model.

Numerical illustration: The displayed graphs use a generated linear schedule: per-person desired hours = max(0, (real wage − intercept)/2). Aggregate hours sum identical individual schedules. Baseline intercept 2 gives 4 individual hours at real wage 10 and 6 at wage 14. A higher-PVLR or higher-leisure-preference illustration uses intercept 4, giving 3 individual hours at wage 10; those are separate reset experiments. Two identical people give aggregate hours 8 at wage 10; three people give 12. Graphs, point guides, labels and cards are calculated from this same schedule. This toy formula is not a formula the professor requires you to memorize.

Source care: SRC-012 slide25 shows PVLR falling and supply shifting right. This lesson also teaches the reverse higher-PVLR/leftward case. Slide34 discusses average empirical evidence; it is not imposed as a universal sign in every hypothetical. Review p.8 compresses the horizon rule into “income effect only appears when permanent”; the lecture slide23 explains the actual simplifying assumption that temporary changes have negligible PVLR effects.
