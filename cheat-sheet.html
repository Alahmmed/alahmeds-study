1. Prices, inflation and real output

πt = (Pt/Pt−1 − 1) × 100%

P = price index; π = inflation. Divide the change by the earlier level.

Nominal GDP = Σ ptqt; real GDP = Σ pbaseqt

p = price/good; q = quantity; Σ adds goods. Real uses base-year prices.

Fixed-basket index = (costt/costbase) × 100

Keep basket quantities fixed. Inflation uses the index change relative to its earlier level.

GDP deflator = (nominal GDP / real GDP) × 100

Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ (deflator/100). CPI uses a consumer basket.

greal = (1+gnominal)/(1+πGDP) − 1

Decimal rates. Approximation: real growth ≈ nominal growth − GDP inflation.

Module 1: SRC-011 slides 52-57; SRC-129 p.2; SRC-079 pp.1,3-5.

2. Interest and purchasing power

Purchasing quantity = dollars / price per good

$100 ÷ $2 per good = 50 goods. Keep dollars and goods distinct.

re ≈ i − πe; ra ≈ i − πa

i = nominal rate; e = expected; a = actual. π (pi) = inflation.

1+ra = (1+i)/(1+πa)

Exact realized return; use decimal rates. Subtraction is an approximation.

Fixed-dollar loan: actual π > expected π

Lower realized real repayment: borrower gains, lender loses relative to expectation.

Module 1: SRC-011 slides 59-62; SRC-129 p.2. Exact formula: derived.

3. Wage and after-tax reward

w = W/P

W = money wage/hour; P = pre-tax goods price. w = goods/hour.

R = [(1 − tn)/(1 + tc)] × w

t_n = labor tax; t_c = consumption tax. Rates in decimals; R = after-tax goods/hour.

Labor tax: 10 × .75 = 7.5; sales tax: 10/1.25 = 8

Separate 25% tax examples at fixed pre-tax real wage 10.

Module 10: SRC-012 slide 26; SRC-083 pp.2-4. Combined-tax accounting: derived.

4. Individual and aggregate labor

Ns(w) = Σi hi(w)

Add each person’s offered hours at the same wage.

Equal individual hours: Ns = pop × h

pop = working-age population; h = offered hours/person.

Population ↓ ⇒ aggregate supply ←

Remaining individual schedules may stay unchanged. More attractive leisure can shift both left.

Module 10: SRC-012 slides 21,26,35; SRC-083 pp.7-8,11. Sum notation: generated.

5. Read the labor-market graph

Vertical: w = W/P; horizontal: N

N = total labor hours. Demand slopes down; course supply slopes up with lifetime resources fixed.

Nd(w*) = Ns(w*) = N*

Stars = equilibrium. Intersection gives w* and N*.

Same w, fewer supplied hours ⇒ supply LEFT

Wage alone moves along supply. A,K fixed ⇒ demand unchanged.

Modules 10-11: SRC-012 slides 19-29,35,52; SRC-083 p.2.

6. Tax and labor-supply shifts

Temporary tax ↑: Ns ←; N* ↓; w* ↑; R ↓

Set lifetime-resource effect aside; A,K and other shifters fixed; no offsetting transfer.

Permanent tax ↑: substitution ←; income →

Lower work reward favors leisure; lower lifetime resources favor work, assuming normal leisure.

Net N*: ↓, unchanged, or ↑

Compare effect strengths. Exact offset: same N*,w*, but R lower.

Module 10: SRC-012 slides 23,25-27,32-34; SRC-131; SRC-083 pp.2-6.

7. Keep the scenarios separate

More attractive leisure ⇒ individual hours ↓

With this change across households, aggregate supply also shifts left.

Supply ← + demand fixed ⇒ N* ↓; w* ↑

For population/leisure examples, reset taxes and lifetime resources; hold A,K fixed.

Supply shifters: PVLR, taxes, population, VL

PVLR = present value of lifetime resources; VL = value of leisure.

Module 10: SRC-012 slides 21-27,35; SRC-083 pp.7-8,11.

8. Output, potential output and LRAS

Y = A K0.3N0.7; Y* = A K0.3(N*)0.7

A = productivity; K = real capital; N* = market-clearing hours.

Y/N = A(K/N)0.3

Output per labor hour. Fixed A,K: N ↓ gives Y ↓ but Y/N ↑.

LRAS: P vertical; Y horizontal; Y = Y*

Flexible money wages: doubling P and W leaves W/P,N*,Y* fixed.

A,K or N* change ⇒ LRAS can shift

Potential is equilibrium output, not a physical maximum. P alone does not shift LRAS.

Sticky wage can leave actual Y < current Y*

Lower market-clearing N* alone is not cyclical unemployment.

Module 11: SRC-012 slides 4,8,52-59; SRC-129 p.13.

The combined-tax purchasing-power expression and individual-hour sum are explanatory accounting notation. Worked numbers are generated illustrations. The supply curve separates the current substitution effect from lifetime resources. Temporary examples set the lifetime-resource effect aside.
