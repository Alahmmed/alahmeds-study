Assumptions and scope: Numerical PIH examples assume zero real interest, unrestricted borrowing, equal desired consumption over the stated remaining periods, no bequest and disposable income as stated. Equal spending is not implied by a budget alone. The supplemental log-utility model gives C_f = β(1+r)C; equal spending follows when β = 1 and r = 0. Borrowing restrictions are studied in Module 16. This lesson does not import AD/SRAS or later price models.

One numerical budget: The professor’s original two-period example has income 1 and 9, zero starting wealth, consumption 5 each, and income saving Y−C of −4 then +4. The temporary surprise adds 2 today only: income 3 and 9, consumption 6 each, current income saving −3. The permanent surprise separately adds 2 in both periods: income 3 and 11, consumption 7 each, current income saving −4. The one-time initial-asset gain of 2 separately gives consumption 6 each. Do not stack separate experiments.

Stocks, flows and denominators: Income saving S = Y−C excludes initial wealth. End-of-period net assets equal initial net wealth plus income saving. After the two-dollar gift, these are −5 and −3 respectively. SRC-143 slide 25 reports the former; SRC-086’s first-period asset-accounting definition includes initial wealth. They are explicitly distinguished here. For an asset gift with unchanged income, ΔC/ΔY is undefined because ΔY = 0. The wealth response is ΔC/Δa. Starting wealth a is not the Keynesian intercept a₀.

Generated response graph: Four annual periods remain. The horizontal axis is extra current disposable income ΔY, and the vertical axis is extra current consumption ΔC. Each axis is in dollars. For the one-time income experiment, ΔC = ΔY/4. For the permanent-income experiment, the same ΔY arrives in every remaining year, so ΔC = ΔY. The two lines compare different specified experiments. At ΔY = 1,000, they give 250 and 1,000 dollars. Every coordinate, guide and card uses these same equations. This is not a labor-market or aggregate-demand diagram.
